This comparison is a UPSC and school-geography staple for good reason: almost every other fact about an Indian river — whether it floods seasonally, whether it forms a delta or an estuary, whether it's been used for major irrigation canals — traces back to which of these two categories it belongs to.

## Origin and water source

Himalayan rivers — the [Ganga](/river/ganga), [Brahmaputra](/river/brahmaputra), and [Indus](/river/indus) and their tributaries — originate from glaciers and snowfields in the high Himalayas. That gives them two water sources: snowmelt (steady, year-round) and monsoon rain (seasonal, heavy). Peninsular rivers rise from much lower hill ranges — the Western Ghats, the Vindhyas, the Satpuras — with no glacial contribution at all, so monsoon rainfall is their only real source.

## Flow pattern

This difference in source shows up directly in flow behavior. Himalayan rivers are perennial: even in peak summer, glacial melt keeps water moving. Peninsular rivers are seasonal — full and often flood-prone during the monsoon, considerably reduced the rest of the year. The [Godavari](/river/godavari) and [Krishna](/river/krishna) can drop to a fraction of their monsoon volume by April and May.

## Valley shape and course

Himalayan rivers are geologically young relative to the mountains they drain, and they're still actively cutting downward — producing steep gorges, meanders, and, historically, frequent course changes across the plains (a major factor in why Bihar and Assam see recurring flood damage). Peninsular rivers flow through much older, more stable terrain, generally following fixed, well-graded valleys with far less lateral movement over time.

## River mouths: delta vs. estuary

Because Himalayan rivers carry a heavy silt load from the mountains, they tend to build deltas at the coast — the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta (shared with Bangladesh) is the largest in the world. Most west-flowing peninsular rivers, by contrast, enter the sea through estuaries rather than deltas: the [Narmada](/river/narmada) and [Tapi](/river/tapi) flow through rift valleys with steeper gradients and less silt deposition at the mouth, so the river simply widens into the sea rather than branching.
