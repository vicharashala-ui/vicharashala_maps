India's rivers fall into two broad families, and the distinction matters for almost everything else you'll read about them: where they begin, how they behave through the year, and how they've shaped the states they pass through.

## Himalayan rivers

Rivers born in the Himalayas — the [Ganga](/river/ganga), [Brahmaputra](/river/brahmaputra), and [Indus](/river/indus) systems — are perennial. They're fed by snowmelt and glaciers as well as monsoon rain, so they carry water year-round even in the dry season. They also tend to be long, with large basins and heavy silt loads, which is why the plains they cross (the Indo-Gangetic plain, most obviously) are some of the most fertile and densely populated land in the country.

The Ganga rises at Gaumukh in Uttarakhand and is joined along its course by major tributaries including the [Yamuna](/river/yamuna), [Gandak](/river/gandak), [Kosi](/river/kosi), and [Son](/river/son) before it reaches the Bay of Bengal through the Sundarbans delta. The Brahmaputra takes a longer, stranger path — it starts in Tibet (where it's called the Yarlung Tsangpo), loops around the eastern Himalayas, and enters India through Arunachal Pradesh before flowing across Assam.

## Peninsular rivers

South of the Vindhya-Satpura range, the picture changes. Peninsular rivers like the [Godavari](/river/godavari), [Krishna](/river/krishna), [Mahanadi](/river/mahanadi), and [Kaveri](/river/kaveri) rise in the Western Ghats and are rain-fed rather than snow-fed — their flow tracks the monsoon closely, running much lower in summer. Most flow eastward into the Bay of Bengal, following the general tilt of the peninsula. The [Narmada](/river/narmada) and [Tapi](/river/tapi) are the notable exceptions, flowing west into the Arabian Sea through rift valleys rather than following the peninsula's eastward slope.

## Why the distinction matters

This split explains a lot of downstream detail: why Himalayan-river states worry about glacial melt and flooding while peninsular states plan around monsoon-dependent irrigation; why the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta supports such intensive agriculture; and why interstate water-sharing disputes (Kaveri, Krishna) are concentrated in peninsular India, where every river's flow is a fixed, rain-dependent quantity rather than a year-round supply.
