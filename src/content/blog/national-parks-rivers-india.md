A river doesn't just pass through a landscape — in many places, it defines the landscape a protected area exists to conserve. Floodplains, deltas, and riverbanks create habitat types that can't exist anywhere else, and a look at India's national parks makes the pattern obvious.

## Floodplain grasslands: the Brahmaputra system

Kaziranga National Park, on the south bank of the [Brahmaputra](/river/brahmaputra) in Assam, is the clearest example. The park's tall grasslands and wetlands (*beels*) are a direct product of the river's annual flooding, which deposits silt and resets the vegetation cycle each year — the same flooding that makes the park famous for its one-horned rhinoceros population. Further along the same system, the [Manas](/river/manas) river feeds Manas National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site straddling the India-Bhutan border.

## Ravine and riverbank habitat: the Chambal

The [Chambal](/river/chambal) river cuts through badlands topography in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh, and the National Chambal Sanctuary follows its course rather than a fixed area on a map. The river's clean, undammed stretches support the gharial (a critically endangered fish-eating crocodilian) and the Gangetic river dolphin — species that depend on the river itself, not just the land beside it.

## Delta mangroves: the Godavari and the Sundarbans

Where a river reaches the coast, the habitat shifts again. The [Godavari](/river/godavari) delta's Coringa Wildlife Sanctuary protects one of India's largest mangrove ecosystems outside the Sundarbans, which itself sits at the joint delta of the Ganga and Brahmaputra systems — mangrove forest exists there specifically because of the mix of freshwater river flow and tidal seawater.
